Restructure a draft page

8 minutes · solo or in pairs

The situation

Riverside Public Works has posted its leaf collection update. Everything on the page is accurate. It is also close to unusable, and every problem on it was introduced by whoever typed it into the CMS.

  1. Open the page in a new tab. Keep this tab for your answers.
  2. No inspector needed. On the page, choose Show how this page is built. Every piece gets a chip with its real tag, and hovering or tabbing to it shows what a screen reader announces.
  3. Work through the seven questions below without looking at the corrected version.
  4. Write down your answers. Nothing is saved, so copy anything you want to keep before you close the tab.
  5. When you are done, open the answer key at the bottom of this page.

Answer key

Open when you are done

1. What the headings told you

The original outline is two headings deep and reads: “Leaf and Yard Waste Collection”, then “Questions?”. Everything between those two is invisible to anyone navigating by heading. A screen reader user pulling up the heading list gets the page title and a question, and no way to jump to the schedule, the bagging rules, or the zone change.

2. The fake headings

Two: “NOTICE TO RESIDENTS” and “Additional Information”. Each is a bolded, enlarged paragraph. They look like headings and carry no structure at all. The all-caps treatment on the first one is a second problem on the same line: some screen readers spell capitalised words out letter by letter, and everyone else reads capitals more slowly.

WCAG 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (A)

3. The heading level problem

The one real heading below the H1 is an H4. That skips levels 2 and 3. A listener hearing “heading level 4” after “heading level 1” has to work out whether they missed two sections. Nothing on the page justifies level 4; it was chosen because the author wanted smaller text.

4. The nine facts in one paragraph

The nine: start date, end date, collection frequency, the Westfield annexation, the zone change, bag type, where to get bags, where to place bags, and the citation risk. Plus the four-zone schedule, which is a tenth. Sorted:

ContentBelongs in
Start and end dates, frequencyA sentence: the opening one
Westfield moved to Zone 3A sentence, in its own section high on the page
The four zones and their daysA table with a caption and header cells
How to bag and set outA numbered list, because order matters
What is and isn’t acceptedA list. Two bulleted ones, in fact
Ordinance 2024-17 and the fineA sentence at the end of the page

5. What a resident needs first

The start date and the zone change. In the original they are in the middle of a 270-word paragraph, about 60 and 90 words in. Both belong in the first sentence. The corrected page puts them in a highlighted lede.

6. The rewrite

Leaf collection runs November 2 through December 31, 2026. We collect every two weeks by zone. Zone assignments changed this year, so check your zone before your first collection day.

Three sentences, 30 words, and a resident can act on it. The original took 270 words to deliver less. The other three sentences on offer are all true: one is preamble with no fact in it, one is a set-out instruction for the numbered list, and one is the ordinance, which goes last.

7. The spacing problem

Empty paragraphs. The original uses four of them to push content down. Some screen readers announce each as “blank”, and they carry no meaning. Spacing is a visual style, so ask for a style.

What was missing from the original

Nothing was added in the rewrite that wasn’t in the source, with one exception: the corrected page states plainly that crews make one pass and don’t return for late material. That was buried in the second paragraph of the original, where nobody would find it before their leaves sat out for two weeks.
